# The statistical analysis

## The claim

A median closure time computed the obvious way, over **closed** cases only, leaves out every
case still open — and the cases still open are disproportionately the slow ones. That is
right-censoring, and dropping censored observations biases the estimate downward. The
correction is the standard one from survival analysis.

So the comparison throughout is **median among closed cases** versus **estimate including open
cases** (Kaplan–Meier). We do not claim this is how the city computes anything: Boston publishes
no 311 median, and CityScore's per-service on-time measures do not document how open cases are
counted. Sources are in [`research/claim-check.md`](research/claim-check.md).

Current figures for every service are generated by `scripts/refresh_numbers.py` into
[`research/NUMBERS.md`](research/NUMBERS.md) and [`../data/numbers.json`](../data/numbers.json).
The tables below are from the run at 2026-10-24T18:45Z (extract cutoff 09:01 UTC).

## The data

`data.boston.gov`, CKAN resource `254adca6-64ab-4c5c-9fc0-a6da622be185`. 29 columns, including
`open_date`, `close_date`, `target_close_date`, `on_time` and `closure_reason`. No API key, and
it sends `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`, so the browser queries it directly and this project
has no backend.

The portal's starter kit ships a 5,000-row extract with 10 columns and no closure timestamps.
The live resource has 75,851 rows. The analysis needs `close_date`, so it uses the live
resource.

### Why the cohort starts 2026-07-01

The resource spans 2025-08-01 to 2026-10-24, but it is **not a complete record of that
period**. Monthly case counts:

| Month | Cases |
| --- | --- |
| 2025-08 | 539 |
| 2025-09 | 418 |
| 2025-10 | 775 |
| 2026-03 | 1,787 |
| 2026-05 | 3,212 |
| 2026-06 | 6,067 |
| 2026-07 | 13,317 |
| 2026-08 | 15,411 |
| 2026-09 | 15,573 |
| 2026-10 | 12,496 (24 days) |

Sep-2026 has 37x the cases of Sep-2025. Boston's 311 volume did not grow 37-fold in a year.
The early months retain only cases that stayed open or were recently touched, which means they
are a sample **selected on survival** — exactly the cases that inflate a duration estimate.
That is left truncation on top of the censoring the analysis is already about.

July 2026 onward is internally consistent at 13–15k cases a month, so the cohort is **cases
with `open_date >= 2026-07-01`**: n = 56,797. Maximum observable duration is about 115 days,
and the app says so rather than implying it can see longer waits.

This restriction invalidated the "longest wait" callouts from an earlier pass. They came from
the contaminated tail and are not used anywhere.

## Methods

**Kaplan–Meier** for time-to-closure. Events are closed cases timed by `close_date`; cases
still in progress are censored at the **extract cutoff** (the latest timestamp in the data),
not at the time the page is viewed. The city refreshes the extract about once a day, so using
"now" would add hours to every open case and inflate the estimate. Greenwood's formula gives the variance for a
confidence band. Where the survival function never falls to 0.5, the median is reported as
undefined rather than substituted with the largest observed time.

**Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence** for how cases close. The 311 data dictionary defines
`Noted` as "no action is required" and `Transitioned` as "reassigned to another agency". Neither
is a `Resolved` closure, so they are a **competing event**, not censoring: treating them as
censored would assume such a case would later close as resolved in this system, which it
cannot. Cause-specific cumulative incidence separates the two outcomes without that assumption.
It does not say whether a transitioned case was later handled by the other agency; the data
cannot show that.

**Wilson score intervals** for on-time proportions, because the normal approximation fails at
proportions near 0 and 1 and several neighborhood-service cells sit there. Cells below n = 30
are suppressed rather than shown with a wide interval.

**Benjamini–Hochberg** correction on any set of group comparisons. With roughly two dozen
neighborhoods, uncorrected pairwise testing would manufacture significance.

Cohort of 56,797 cases opened on or after 2026-07-01:

| --- | --- |
| Closed | 48,903 |
| Still open | 7,876 |
| Still open and past the city's own target date | **6,624** |
| Closed as `Noted` or `Transitioned` | 11,701 of 48,903 — **23.9%** |

"Past target" is computed from `target_close_date` against the extract cutoff, not from the

cases the flag lags: 384 open cases already past their target still read ONTIME, 324 of them
Street Light Outage. Open cases not yet due are left out of both on-time figures because their
outcome is undecided.

Per service the second figure moves a lot: Street Light Outage is 75.1% on time among closed
cases and 53.5% once open cases past target count as late; Pothole is 38.1% and 29.8%, against
CityScore's stated aim of 80% within one business day.

## Closure forecast

**Question.** A request has been open *N* whole days. What is the historical chance it closes
in the next 7? Both methods give `P = 1 − S(N+7) / S(N)`, where closure means any closure,
including Noted or Transitioned. The "Already waiting?" block under the main answer shows the
service-wide figure from the live Kaplan–Meier curve. A neighborhood-aware model was allowed
next to it only if it beat that figure on later requests.

**Model.** A Cox proportional-hazards model (lifelines `CoxPHFitter`) with service as a
**stratum** and one-hot neighborhood covariates (23 levels in training, 24 in the final fit,
plus "Other or unknown"), with a ridge penalty of 0.001. Service is a stratum rather than a
covariate because each service gets its own baseline curve (litter closes in hours, street
lights in weeks), so the comparison with per-service Kaplan–Meier measures only what
neighborhood adds. The only predictors are fields known at filing; closure reason and `on_time`
are not used. A KNN regressor averaging closed cases was ruled out because it would rebuild the
closed-cases-only bias this project measures. Code: `scripts/train_closure_forecast.py`.

**Test.** Fit on cases opened 1 July–31 August, censored at 1 September 00:00 UTC (27,851
cases in the 28 site services that existed then). Scored on cases opened 1–17 September,
followed to the 24 October extract cutoff, at *N* = 0, 14 and 30 days. At each *N* a case
counts only if it was still open at *N* and its follow-up could reach *N* + 7 days. That rule
uses open time only, never the outcome, so no inverse-probability weighting was needed.
11,419 case-day checks in all. The gate was fixed in the script before any result was seen.

| Days already waited | Checks | Closed within 7 d | Brier, KM | Brier, Cox | Change (95% bootstrap) | Neighborhood alone |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 9,311 | 8,069 | 0.0738 | 0.0736 | −0.3% (−0.6 to +0.1) | −0.2% |
| 14 | 1,131 | 54 | 0.0496 | 0.0459 | −7.5% (−15.1 to −1.8) | +0.1% |

"Neighborhood alone" compares the Cox model with its own service-only baseline (Nelson–Aalen,
no neighborhood). It shows that the gains at 14 and 30 days are **not** from neighborhood. They
come from estimating the curve as exp(−cumulative hazard) instead of as a product-limit, which
matters where few cases remain at risk; the service-only Nelson–Aalen gets the same gain. The
neighborhood terms change the Brier score by 0.2% at most.

So the page keeps the service-wide Kaplan–Meier figure, and the neighborhood control stays
hidden. The artifact records this (`release.passed: false`), and the page reads that flag rather
than a hard-coded switch. A rerun that passes would turn the control on with no code change.
`?forecast=preview` shows the control, labelled as a preview, for review only. The 14- and
30-day results point at a separate, untested change: estimating the service-wide curve with
Nelson–Aalen. That needs its own pre-registered test before it replaces anything.

**Assumptions, checked before reading any coefficient.** A rank-time Schoenfeld test flags
none of the 24 neighborhood terms (largest statistic 1.8). But refitting on the first 7 days
and then on days after 7 (left-truncated at day 7; 3,774 cases, 932 closures) gives neighborhood
effects that barely agree: correlation 0.10, the same sign for 17 of 23, and shifts of up to
0.50 in log hazard ratio. The effects are small (hazard ratios 0.81–1.20 against the average
neighborhood) and not stable over time, so **no neighborhood coefficient is interpreted here**.
As elsewhere in this project, nothing here supports a causal or income claim.

**Static artifact.** After validation the script refits on the full cohort (54,721 cases in 33
services, censored at the extract cutoff) and writes
[`public/model/boston-closure-forecast.json`](../public/model/boston-closure-forecast.json)
(87 KB). It holds only coefficients, feature names and means, each service's baseline survival
on a daily grid, per-day at-risk counts, neighborhood case counts, the training date and the
gate result. It has no case-level records. Before writing it, the script checks that the
exported numbers reproduce lifelines' own predictions (to 5e-12). The page falls back to the
service-wide figure if the gate failed, the model is more than 30 days old, the neighborhood is
missing, the service has fewer than 30 training cases there or fewer than 30 still open at day
*N*, or *N* + 7 is past the curve. Every metric is in
[`../data/forecast_evaluation.json`](../data/forecast_evaluation.json).

Boston only. A Cambridge view, if one is added, would keep its own method: its data has
different fields and closure timing.

## Limitations

- **Independent censoring.** Kaplan–Meier assumes cases still open are independent of eventual
  closure time. If the city defers difficult cases, the estimate is *optimistic* and real waits
  are longer than reported. There is no test for this in the data.
- **No causal claims.** The data cannot say why one service or area is slower. Case density,
  street-light ownership (city, utility or private) and contractor routing are all confounded.
- **No neighborhood or income claims.** An earlier pass proposed an equity-gap framing built
  on an invented statistic. It was checked, did not hold up, and was dropped. Neighborhood
  comparisons in this project are descriptive only, with small cells suppressed.
- **Prevalence, not a rate.** "6,624 open cases are past their target date" is a
  cross-sectional count over cases of differing ages. It has no denominator.
- **Horizon.** The cohort cannot speak to waits beyond about 115 days.

## Verification

`scripts/refresh_numbers.py` recomputes every figure from the live endpoint with lifelines,
re-checks the four headline services against the independent Python reference, checks that
the cohort months are still even and the extract is fresh, and fails if a live document uses a
claim we cannot source. Run it before recording.

`scripts/reference_impl.py` is an independently written Python implementation. It produces
`src/lib/__fixtures__/golden.json` from the real cohort, and `src/lib/stats.test.ts` asserts
that the TypeScript reproduces those values. Two implementations written separately agreeing is
a stronger check than either one's own unit tests.

`scripts/train_closure_forecast.py` also writes `src/lib/__fixtures__/forecastExamples.json`:
80 Cox predictions from the fitted lifelines model and 20 Kaplan–Meier conditional estimates
from lifelines on the committed case fixture. `src/lib/closureForecast.test.ts` asserts that
the TypeScript reader, using only the exported artifact, matches them to 1e-6 (Cox) and 1e-9
(Kaplan–Meier). It also tests every fallback rule, plus three fast-check properties.
